# Catapult Project

## Situation
In my AP Physics 1 class we were given a competition. Each team had to build a catapult and launch a pumpkin, and whichever team sent theirs the farthest won. Two rules shaped the entire design. No one could touch the catapult at the moment of launch, and the whole build had to fit inside a footprint of 1.5 feet by 1 foot.

## Task
I took on two roles. I organized the team, which meant assigning everyone a job based on what they were suited to and running check ins during every class period to make sure we were still on pace. I also built the physical catapult. A teammate handled the calculations, and my job was turning those numbers into something that existed and worked.

## Action
I assigned roles early rather than letting the work sort itself out, because a build with a hard deadline goes wrong when two people do the same task and nobody does a third one. From there the check ins were what kept the project moving. Asking each person where they were every class meant a problem surfaced while there was still time to fix it instead of the day before the launch.

We built the catapult from wood and screws, using rubber bands as the energy source. Drawing the throwing arm back stretches the rubber bands and stores energy in them, and releasing the arm converts that stored energy into the motion that throws the pumpkin. Rubber bands were the right choice for the scale we were working at, since they store a useful amount of energy, they are easy to add or remove to adjust the force, and they do not require any of the machining that a spring system would. The footprint limit also mattered here. With the base and the arm both capped by the 1.5 foot by 1 foot boundary, we could not get distance by simply building something bigger, so the distance had to come from how much energy we stored and how much of it actually reached the pumpkin.

The no touching rule is what drove the release mechanism. We could not simply hold the arm and let go, so I built a string release. The arm is held under tension until someone pulls the string from a distance, which frees the arm and launches the pumpkin without anyone making contact with the catapult itself. That constraint turned out to be the most interesting part of the build, because it meant the design had to account not just for throwing the pumpkin but for how the throw would be triggered.

Testing is where the real problem showed up. The catapult has a bar that stops the throwing arm at the end of its swing, which is the moment the pumpkin separates from the arm and keeps traveling on its own. When we tested it, that stopping bar was not stiff enough and flexed on impact instead of holding firm.

That flex was costing us distance. Energy that bends a piece of wood is energy that never reaches the pumpkin, so every launch was falling short of what the stored energy in the rubber bands should have produced. The fix was reinforcing the stopping bar so it could arrest the arm without giving way. Once the bar stopped absorbing the impact, more of that energy transferred into the pumpkin and our launches went noticeably farther.

Working from a teammate's calculations rather than my own meant I had to understand what the numbers were describing before I could build to them. A figure on paper does not tell you how thick the arm needs to be or where a screw should go, so translating the math into a physical object was its own problem.

## Result
Our catapult launched the pumpkin 13 feet, which was farther than any other team in the class, and we won the competition. The pumpkin was roughly six inches in diameter, so the mechanism had to handle a payload with real size and weight rather than something small and light.

This was the first time I used physics to build a real thing that worked. Up to that point physics had been problems on paper, and this project was where the equations turned into an object sitting in front of me doing what the math said it would do.

The other thing I took from it was leadership. Assigning roles and running check ins was not something the assignment required of me, and doing it is a large part of why we finished on time and won. Managing a team turned out to be a skill of its own, separate from the building.
